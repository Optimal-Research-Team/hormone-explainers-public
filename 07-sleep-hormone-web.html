    A web, not a line

    Sleep, melatonin, cortisol, insulin and the sex hormones pull on each other in both directions. Each numbered line is one connection.

    Hover any hormone or number in the diagram to trace it.

    Start with sleep

    1Sleep ↔ Melatonin

    Melatonin signals night to the body and helps time sleep; light in the evening suppresses it. Melatonin output and deep sleep both tend to decline with age.

    2Sleep ↔ Cortisol

    Short or broken sleep can raise evening cortisol, blunting the usual evening drop, and chronic insomnia is linked with higher cortisol and ACTH around bedtime. In turn, an overactive stress system in the evening goes with lighter, more broken sleep, which seems to be driven more by the brain’s stress signals than by cortisol itself.

    4Sleep ↔ Insulin resistance

    Less sleep lowers insulin sensitivity and raises type 2 diabetes risk. Insulin resistance often travels with excess weight and obstructive sleep apnea, which breaks up sleep in turn.

    Melatonin and cortisol

    3Melatonin ↔ Cortisol

    Their daily rhythms run in opposite directions: melatonin rises in the evening as cortisol falls, and fades toward morning as cortisol climbs. When the body clock is disrupted, by night shifts or bright evening light for example, both rhythms tend to lose their shape.

    Insulin resistance

    5Insulin resistance ↔ Cortisol

    Cortisol raises blood sugar, reduces glucose uptake by muscle and fat, and can blunt β-cell insulin release, so long-term high cortisol promotes insulin resistance. Some studies suggest high insulin can in turn stimulate the stress axis.

    6Insulin resistance → Testosterone

    In women, high insulin boosts androgen production in the ovaries (alongside LH) and lowers SHBG, leaving more free testosterone: part of the pattern in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), with acne or excess hair. In men, insulin resistance tends to lower testosterone instead.

    7Insulin resistance → Estrogen

    Body fat contains aromatase, which turns androgens into estrogen, and lower SHBG leaves more of it free. After menopause, fat tissue becomes the main source of estrogen.

    Cortisol and the sex hormones

    8Testosterone ↔ Cortisol

    Testosterone tends to damp the stress axis, shown mainly in animal studies and men. Chronically high cortisol suppresses the reproductive axis and can lower testosterone.

    9Cortisol → Progesterone

    Chronic stress can lower progesterone by disrupting ovulation, since progesterone is made mainly after ovulation. Less progesterone means less allopregnanolone, which may add to anxiety and poorer sleep.

    10Estrogen ↔ Cortisol

    Oral estrogen, including the pill and oral hormone therapy, raises the protein that carries cortisol, so total cortisol on a blood test reads higher. Animal studies suggest estrogen can amplify the stress response; high cortisol can in turn disrupt the menstrual cycle.

    What reaches mood

    11Cortisol, sleep → Mood

    Long-term high cortisol and poor sleep are linked to changes in brain areas for memory, focus and emotion: the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex and amygdala. Some of these changes can reverse once cortisol returns to normal.

    12Sex hormones → Mood

    Testosterone, progesterone and estrogen all shape mood and cognition. Shifting levels, as in perimenopause, can affect mood, sleep and memory.

    Sleep is a good place to start

    Sleep connects directly to melatonin, cortisol and insulin resistance, and through them to the rest of the web.

    Ongoing insomnia or loud snoring with pauses in breathing is worth raising with a clinician: both are common and treatable.
