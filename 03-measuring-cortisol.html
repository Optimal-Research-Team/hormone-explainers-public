Three ways to measure cortisol

Cortisol changes hour by hour, so researchers often describe the shape of the whole day rather than one value. These three research measures are all read from the same curve.

    Read the whole curve

    This is an illustrative day of salivary cortisol, from waking to bedtime. In research, the curve is described by its shape:

    How sharply it rises on waking, how steadily it falls, and how much is produced in total.

    1Cortisol awakening response

    The rise in the first 30 to 45 minutes after waking. Researchers think it helps the body get ready for the day ahead.

    Clear rise · typicalSmaller rise · blunted

    2Diurnal slope

    The drop from the morning peak to the evening low (D − N). A steady decline is part of winding down for sleep.

    Clear drop · typicalLittle drop · flatter

    3Area under the curve

    The whole shaded area: roughly how much cortisol is made across the day.

    Mid range · typicalHigher or lower total

    When the curve flattens

    A smaller rise and little drop across the day. Depending on the cause, the day’s total can end up higher or lower. In research, flatter slopes are linked with chronic stress and poorer health on average.

    Research measures, not a diagnosis

    Salivary cortisol curves vary from day to day with sleep, the timing of each sample and how it’s collected. Even a 5 to 15 minute delay in taking the first sample can make the morning rise look bigger or smaller than it really is.

    They aren’t a validated way to diagnose stress-related conditions. If a clinician uses them at all, it’s alongside your history and other tests.

    Cortisol disorders such as Cushing’s syndrome and adrenal insufficiency are diagnosed with specific, standardized blood, urine or late-night saliva tests.
