Chapter 02Stress and rhythm

How chronic stress can change the cortisol curve

Cortisol follows a daily rhythm. Research links long-term stress and poorer health with a flatter version of that curve. Here is what that looks like, and what it doesn’t mean.

    02 · Stress and rhythm

    Two systems respond to stress

    SNSSeconds
    Sympathetic nervous system→Adrenal medulla→Adrenaline
    HPAMinutes to hours
    Hypothalamus→Pituitary→Adrenal cortex→Cortisol

    Both are normal, healthy responses. The HPA axis switches itself off through negative feedback. On the right is cortisol’s typical daily rhythm.

    Typical rhythm

    Cortisol climbs quickly in the first 30 to 45 minutes after waking, then falls across the day, reaching its low point around midnight, early in the night’s sleep.

    Once the job is done, cortisol’s own negative feedback switches off CRH and ACTH upstream.

    Short-term stress

    During a demanding stretch, both stress systems work harder and cortisol can run higher through the day and into the evening.

    This is a normal, adaptive response. The rhythm usually settles once the pressure eases.

    Longer-term stress

    When stress goes on for months, some people’s curves flatten: morning cortisol runs lower and evening cortisol higher, so the day’s total can end up higher than usual.

    Animal studies suggest that under repeated stress a second brain signal, arginine vasopressin (AVP), takes a bigger part in driving ACTH. How far this applies in people is still unclear.

    Flattened rhythm

    Some studies find lower, flatter curves in people with long-standing stress, PTSD or chronic fatigue syndrome, though results are mixed.

    A flat curve is a pattern, not a diagnosis. It doesn’t mean the adrenal glands have “run out”.

    Adrenal fatigue isn’t a recognized medical diagnosis (Endocrine Society).

    Patterns seen in research

    Side by side, the four curves show patterns reported in research, not stages everyone passes through. A 2017 meta-analysis linked flatter daytime cortisol slopes with poorer mental and physical health.

    These are group averages. Any one person’s curve also shifts from day to day with sleep, timing and routine.

    SourcesAdam et al., Psychoneuroendocrinology 2017 (diurnal slope meta-analysis); Miller, Chen and Zhou, Psychol Bull 2007 (chronic stress and a high, flat profile); Fries et al., Psychoneuroendocrinology 2005 (hypocortisolism); Endocrine Society, on adrenal fatigue.
